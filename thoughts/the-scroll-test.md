# the scroll test.

what francis zierer taught me about making a company publication worth reading.

january 23, 2026by shashank sn·10 min read

so many newsletters are a slow-motion car crash. they start with high hopes, hit a wall around issue seven, and disappear before issue ten.

francis zierer was about to ship his 150th issue of creator spotlight, beehiiv’s publication about the people building businesses around content. the part of the conversation i kept returning to had little to do with software.

his job was to make a company publication worth reading on its own.

## start with an editorial job

the early creator spotlight featured beehiiv users. that format gave creators attention and showed what the product enabled.

francis inherited an audience and a wider mandate. the publication needed an editorial reason to exist beyond “here is another customer story.”

marketing collateral begins with what the company wants to say. editorial work begins with what the reader wants to understand.

> the product can be part of the context. it cannot be the reason every sentence exists.

## from user spotlight to publication

platform companies love the spotlight format because it is efficient. the user gets attention, the platform gets proof, and the story can be turned into a case study.

that format stops working when every story follows the same arc. founder joins platform, works hard, grows, recommends platform. the reader can see the machine behind the page.

francis treated the subject as the story. what did this person believe before the audience arrived? what did they get wrong? what did the work cost? what changed when the business became real?

the platform stays in the background and becomes more credible because it lets the story breathe.

creator spotlight began as a familiar platform series. over its first seventy-one issues it grew to roughly 175,000 subscribers. when francis joined, the audience was already there, but the project needed an owner who could turn a side project into a publication with a point of view.

beehiiv’s ceo had described the ambition as building the thing the hustle became for hubspot, except in-house from the beginning. the ambition gave the team direction. the editorial choices still had to make the publication feel like a place rather than a campaign.

## texture is a reading experience

francis used the word texture, and it is a good word for a page.

texture is what happens as your eye moves down: a strong line, a useful quote, a concrete example, a section break, and a moment of visual relief. each element gives the reader another reason to keep going.

tone is the accumulated feeling. it comes from the questions the editor asks, the details they keep, and the language they refuse to flatten.

the scroll test is simple. move through the issue without reading every word. does anything make you stop? can you sense a point of view? does the page have rhythm?

if nothing catches, the issue is asking for commitment before it has earned attention.

## the welcome email is issue zero

most welcome emails are receipts. they confirm the address, promise future value, and vanish.

francis treated the welcome email as the first editorial experience. it showed representative work, explained the publication’s job, and asked readers about the creators and businesses they were following.

that reply has practical value. it can improve inbox placement and supply future interview leads. more importantly, it establishes the relationship correctly: the reader has something to contribute.

the first email should answer one question: what will i get here that i cannot get everywhere else?

the answer cannot be “more tips.” the inbox already has enough tips. it has to be a specific way of seeing the creator economy, a level of access, or a type of story that the reader would miss without you.

## the editor is the product manager

francis chose topics and decided what the publication would reward.

if every story rewarded revenue screenshots, readers learned that revenue was the only interesting outcome. if every story rewarded a tidy origin story, founders hid the messy middle. an editor changes the category by changing what gets attention.

the work is closer to product management than content scheduling. define the job, watch what readers return for, remove what has gone stale, and protect the parts that make the publication recognisable.

## do not confuse volume with a habit

shipping frequently matters because a publication needs to become part of a reader’s week. volume by itself creates noise.

the useful cadence is the one the team can sustain without turning every issue into a smaller version of the last. a slower issue with a real observation can create more memory than five interchangeable summaries.

## consistency is an arrangement

a publication serves several people at once. the reader wants insight. the writer needs a subject they can care about for years. the company wants durable trust. sponsors need attention without wrecking the experience.

the project becomes fragile when one party extracts too much. the writer burns out. the reader stops opening. the sponsor takes over the page. the company turns every story into proof of purchase.

consistency is an arrangement that gives everyone a reason to keep showing up.

## questions worth asking before you publish

01. what does this publication help a reader understand?
02. which parts would still be useful if the product disappeared from the sentence?
03. where does the page make the eye stop?
04. what does the welcome email teach the reader to expect?
05. can the writer keep finding subjects after issue fifty?

these questions do more than improve a newsletter. they prevent a company from building a content machine that nobody wants to enter.

a company can own distribution. it still has to earn readership.
