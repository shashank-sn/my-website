# you don’t know your real competition.

the thing stealing the decision is usually a habit, a fear, or a foggy message.

january 2, 2026by shashank sn·8 min read

a couple of weeks ago i finished reading obviously awesome by april dunford and had an uncomfortable thought: she had somehow written down the thing i keep seeing in client work.

founders think the competitor is the company with the similar feature list. most of the time, the real competition is the customer’s existing habit, confusion, fear, or decision to do nothing.

## the comparison is already a loss

when a prospect places you beside three other providers, the decision becomes a spreadsheet. price, features, reviews, delivery time. you can win that comparison, but you are still playing inside someone else’s frame.

the iPod skipped a comparison of storage, battery life, and file formats. it gave me “1,000 songs in your pocket.” i could see the future immediately. the other options were still on the shelf, but they had stopped mattering.

the iPod was never asking to win a comparison. it made comparison unnecessary.

## the four things you are actually fighting

first is inertia. the buyer already has a workaround, even if it is painful. a spreadsheet, a freelancer, a manual process, or the founder doing it themselves can feel safer than change.

second is confusion. if the customer cannot tell what changes after they buy, every option feels risky. a vague promise creates a price conversation because price is the only clear thing left.

third is internal approval. the person who likes your offer may still need to explain it to a boss, partner, or finance team. if your message cannot travel without you, the deal slows down.

fourth is fear of looking foolish. a customer is buying the outcome and the chance to defend the decision later.

## find the job behind the category

april’s positioning work starts with the customer’s alternatives, rather than the company’s adjectives. what did they use before you? what did they try? what did they reject?

the answers show the real job. a founder may say they need brand strategy, but what they actually need is the confidence to launch a risky product. a team may say they need a dashboard, but what they want is a decision they can make in one meeting.

name that job and you stop sounding like a category page.

## make the right person feel seen

specificity is evidence that you understand the situation.

“we help businesses grow” gives the reader nothing to hold. “we help solo software founders sell globally without becoming tax experts” creates a picture. the second line filters. the right person leans in; everyone else gets permission to leave.

## a test for your next homepage

hide your logo and read the page like someone who has never heard of you. can you answer four questions in one minute?

- 01what changed for the customer?
- 02who is this clearly for?
- 03what did they do before?
- 04why is this a safer decision now?

if the answer is “we are new” or “we offer end-to-end solutions,” the real competitor is still winning. it is the fog.

## stop trying to beat everyone

you can leave “best option for a market” behind. become the obvious option for a particular person in a particular moment.

that means giving up some customers. it means saying no to the version of the business that sounds large but feels forgettable.

the goal is a decision that feels like it was made for them, rather than a louder comparison.
