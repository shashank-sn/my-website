the scroll test.

what francis zierer taught me about making a company publication worth reading.

january 24, 2026by shashank sn·6 min read

company newsletters tend to die in one of two ways.

some become release notes with a friendly introduction. others become generic media publications that could belong to any company. both lose the reason a reader chose them.

francis zierer has spent years editing creator spotlight, beehiiv’s publication about the people building businesses around content. when we spoke, the most useful part of the conversation had little to do with growth tactics.

it was about making the publication worth caring about on its own.

the publication needs a job

the early version of creator spotlight featured beehiiv users. that format gave creators attention and showed what the product enabled.

francis inherited an audience and a wider mandate. his job was to turn a useful company series into a publication with its own editorial reason to exist.

the distinction matters. marketing collateral begins with what the company wants to say. editorial work begins with what the reader wants to understand.

a company publication should be useful before the product enters the sentence.

beehiiv remains part of the context. the publication can still build trust for the company. forcing every story to sell the software would make the work less credible and less readable.

texture is part of the argument

francis described the publication through texture and tone. those words stayed with me.

texture is what happens as the eye moves down the page: a strong line, a section break, a concrete example, a useful quote, and a moment of visual relief. each element gives the reader another reason to continue.

tone is the accumulated feeling. it comes from the questions an editor asks, the details they keep, and the language they refuse to flatten.

the scroll test is simple: move through the issue without reading every word. does anything make you stop? can you sense a point of view? does the page have a rhythm?

if nothing catches, the issue is asking commitment from the reader before it has earned attention.

the welcome email is issue zero

most welcome emails are receipts. they confirm the address, promise future value, and disappear.

francis treated the welcome email as the first editorial experience. it introduced the publication, shared representative work, and asked readers about the creators they followed and the work they were building.

that reply has practical value. it can help inbox placement and supply future interview leads. it also establishes the relationship correctly: the reader has something to contribute.

consistency needs aligned incentives

a publication serves several people at once. the reader wants insight. the writer needs a subject they can care about for years. the company wants durable trust. advertisers need attention without destroying the experience.

the project becomes fragile when one party extracts too much. a writer burns out. a reader stops opening. a sponsor takes over the page. a company turns every story into proof of purchase.

francis kept publishing because he cared about the subject and had room to exercise editorial judgment. that arrangement created consistency; the calendar only recorded it.

the practical test

  1. 01state the editorial job. explain what the reader will understand after spending time with you.
  2. 02run the scroll test. check the rhythm before asking anyone to read closely.
  3. 03treat the welcome as issue zero. show the work and begin a conversation.
  4. 04protect the arrangement. reader, writer, company, and sponsor need a reason to stay.

a company can own distribution. it still has to earn readership.
