field guide / naming

simple guide to naming your company.

i have named three companies and broken most of the naming rules people treat as law.

december 29, 2025by shashank sn·4 min read

i have named three companies.

el mejor coffee was regularly pronounced “el major.” we kept selling coffee.

happy beginnings came from happy endings, which was a terrible place to begin naming a wedding invitation company. the name survived.

stupidpreneur contains the word stupid. people still ask who the stupid one is. my answer is usually anyone building something worth building.

the businesses gave these names their meaning later.

naming can become respectable procrastination

a founder can spend months searching domains, asking friends, and moving the same words around a spreadsheet. it feels like company building because the decision is visible and permanent-looking.

talking to a customer is less comfortable. shipping a rough product is worse. so the name receives weeks of attention while the business remains theoretical.

the name rarely carries the company. the company teaches people what the name means.

three checks are enough to move

  • 01can you say it without apologising or explaining for five minutes?
  • 02can another person hear it, repeat it, and find you with reasonable effort?
  • 03does a basic company, domain, and trademark search reveal an obvious conflict?

the third check deserves actual care. a naming decision should survive legal and market checks in the places where you plan to operate. this is different from expecting the exact dot-com to be cheap and available on day one.

what the radio test misses

people say a good name should be spelled correctly after hearing it once. it is a useful constraint with plenty of exceptions.

if a strange spelling gives you a permanent customer-support problem, walk away. if the name is slightly unusual but easy to learn after one encounter, you probably have a workable name.

clarity around the product can carry a less obvious name. a perfect name cannot rescue a product nobody understands.

give the decision one serious day

my process is deliberately short:

  • 01write a large, bad list before trying to be clever.
  • 02remove conflicts, awkward explanations, and names you cannot say naturally.
  • 03choose the strongest survivor and put it in front of a customer.

you may rename the company later. that costs time, money, memory, and paperwork, so avoid treating it as the plan. the possibility simply proves that this decision is survivable.

if you have spent longer naming the company than speaking to the people it is meant to serve, the name is no longer the problem.
