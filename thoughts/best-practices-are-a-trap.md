# best practices are a trap.

a best practice can remove a disadvantage. it rarely gives you an advantage everybody else can copy.

march 31, 2026by shashank sn·5 min read

a friend once asked me to review his startup’s go-to-market plan.

he had done everything properly. the pricing followed the market. the landing page used the familiar structure. the team had borrowed proven campaigns and hired people who knew the playbook.

the plan had no obvious fault and gave customers no particular reason to choose them.

## parity is useful

shared defaults exist because they have solved a problem more than once. ignoring them automatically is an expensive personality trait.

a familiar checkout reduces confusion. security practices protect customers. accessibility standards let more people use the product. these are places where originality can create damage.

use the default when the default removes unnecessary risk.

the mistake is expecting the same default to create a competitive edge.

> a shared default can remove a disadvantage. it rarely creates an advantage.

## the original problem gets forgotten

a practice begins as an answer to a specific constraint. other companies copy the visible answer. consultants give it a name. the context disappears.

years later, teams follow the practice because serious companies are supposed to follow it.

ask which problem the practice was built to solve and whether you still have that problem.

## investigate the consensus

write down five things your company does because that is how the category works.

take one of them through four questions:

01. which original problem made this practice sensible?

02. what has changed since the practice became common?

03. what would the opposite decision make easier?

04. which new risk would the opposite decision create?

most inversions will be bad. the exercise only needs to reveal the assumption.

## an edge needs a mechanism

doing the opposite becomes strategy only when it creates something valuable.

refusing a common service can make the remaining offer easier to understand. a slower onboarding might catch expensive mistakes. publishing the limitation could attract customers who are tired of inflated promises.

the unusual decision needs a mechanism connecting it to trust, cost, speed, focus, or another outcome the customer can feel.

without that mechanism, “breaking the rules” is decoration.

## keep the boring defaults boring

payroll, password storage, and the button people use to pay you can remain boring.

save the difficult thinking for the assumptions that determine who the company serves, what it refuses, how it makes money, and why a customer should care.

follow the playbook where sameness keeps people safe. question it where sameness makes you disappear.
