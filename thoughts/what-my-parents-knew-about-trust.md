# what my parents knew about trust.

thirty-five years inside a printing press taught me what recommendation really costs.

november 10, 2025by shashank sn·5 min read

i grew up in a tier-two city in tamil nadu watching my parents run a printing press.

they have spent thirty-five years opening the same shop and solving urgent problems long after the novelty disappeared: wedding invitations needed by thursday, school material on an immovable deadline, and customers remembering one missing detail after the plates were ready.

my parents never described any of this as brand building. they were trying to keep promises.

## the dinner-table test

in smaller cities, business moves through conversations that never appear in an analytics dashboard.

a shopkeeper recommends a printer. a doctor mentions a name to a patient. one family sends another family to the same place for their daughter’s wedding invitations. the person making that recommendation puts a little of their own standing on the line.

> a recommendation is borrowed reputation.

my parents understood that instinctively. their working question was simple: what will this customer say about us at dinner tonight?

“they were cheap” dies at the table. “they never let me down” travels.

## reliability compounds quietly

trust was built through small decisions that looked inefficient on a spreadsheet.

my father remembered the names of customers’ children. the press stayed open when a deadline mattered. a mistake was fixed before anyone could turn it into a fight. they showed up at community events because they belonged there, without turning every gathering into a lead-generation exercise.

none of those actions created a dramatic spike. together, they made the business easy to mention and safe to recommend.

years later, clients started bringing their children’s businesses to the same press. that is what compounding trust looks like in real life. the next transaction arrives carrying the memory of the previous hundred.

## the words customers use are the brand

most companies write their positioning from the inside. they choose the qualities they want associated with them and put those words on a website.

the useful language lives outside the company.

call three customers who have recommended you and ask what they told the other person. write the answer exactly as given and leave professional language out of it.

if they say “solid,” find out what solid means. if they say “they handle it,” ask which moment proved that. those stories reveal the promises your operation already knows how to keep.

## what i carried forward

the printing press gave me a definition of brand before i had the vocabulary for it.

brand is the sentence somebody else is willing to say when you are absent. every delivery, apology, deadline, and difficult decision gives that sentence its shape.

my parents built theirs one ordinary day at a time.
